Why we built Thinkfork

Picture a task you do all the time: asking an AI to help you understand a book. You ask for the key ideas of Thinking, Fast and Slow, and the reply gives you five of them:

  1. Two systems of thinking
  2. Heuristics and biases
  3. Prospect theory
  4. Overconfidence
  5. The experiencing self and the remembering self

You are curious about the first one, so you ask about it. The answer has its own sub-points, you pick one, and a few rounds later you are deep in a detail. Then you remember: what was that third idea, prospect theory? It is somewhere far up the chat. You scroll, you skim, you lose your place, and often you just give up. The other four ideas are gone, and you never see the whole book in one view.

The same thing happens when an agent does real work for you. Ask it to plan a software release and the answer has several decisions at once: the version scope, the rollout plan, the pricing change, the announcement. You dive into the rollout, then into one risk inside it, and by the time you are done the other decisions have scrolled out of sight.

Almost every serious conversation looks like this: the answer is a set of points, not a single line, and we follow only one or two of them at a time. A chat window is a single line, so everything we did not follow is buried.

Thinkfork fixes exactly this. Every reply is a card on a canvas, and every follow-up grows a branch from the point you chose. The five ideas stay side by side as five branches. When you want prospect theory, you click that spot and ask there, exactly where you left it, and the earlier answers stay as they were. When you finish, the tree is a map of everything you explored, instead of a long scroll to re-read.

A chat is a line. Your questions aren’t.

Most topics are layered: one big question made of several parts, each with its own follow-ups. A chat window only lets you go down one part at a time, and the rest scrolls away.

Start a session in any folder on your Mac. Your question and the reply appear as cards on the canvas.

Your model, your key

Choose a provider, paste a key, pick a model and a thinking effort. Switch whenever you like.

Local first

Canvases, settings, and your key live on your computer. There is no account and no cloud sync.

Bring the model you already use

Pick a provider in Settings and paste your API key. Nothing else to configure.

  • DeepSeek
  • OpenRouter
  • OpenAI
  • Anthropic
  • Google Gemini
  • Any OpenAI-compatible endpoint

For a custom endpoint, enter its base URL and key. The model list is fetched automatically, and you can add model names by hand if the endpoint doesn’t publish one.

Your conversations stay yours

Thinkfork runs on your Mac. Your conversations go only to the model provider you choose, and we run no server that receives them.

  • Stored on your computer. Canvases and settings are plain files in your user library. Delete the folder and they’re gone.
  • Key never leaves for us. Your API key is sent only to the provider you picked. We run no server that sees it.
  • Closed to other programs. The app’s local service listens only on your machine, on a random port, and requires a secret that only its own window holds.

Questions

Is it free?

Yes. Thinkfork is free to download and use. You only pay your model provider for the requests you make with your own key.

Is there a Windows or Intel Mac version?

Not yet. The first release is for Apple silicon Macs. Other platforms will follow.

Do I need an account?

No. You need an API key from the model provider you want to use, and nothing else.

Where is my data stored?

In ~/Library/Application Support/Thinkfork. Removing that folder removes your canvases and settings.

What is DSH?

DSH is the open-source agent engine that Thinkfork runs locally to talk to models and work with your files. Thinkfork is the canvas interface built around it.
